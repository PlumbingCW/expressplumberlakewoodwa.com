Garbage Disposal Plumbing and Hookup: DIY or Plumber?

The box is open, the old unit is under the sink, and the new disposal is sitting on the counter. Swapping one is a doable Saturday job for many handy homeowners, but the hookup has four spots where small mistakes cause slow leaks, a dead dishwasher drain or a tripped breaker.

This guide walks through each connection, shows where DIY usually works, and names the points where a licensed plumber is the safer call.

By the Lakewood Express Plumbing team. Updated 2026.

The four connections that decide the job

Every disposal hookup comes down to the same four joints. Get them right and the unit runs for a decade. Get one wrong and you find out when the cabinet floor goes soft.

  • 1
    The sink flangeA rubber gasket and plumber's putty seal the flange to the sink. This joint leaks first if the mounting ring is not tightened evenly.
  • 2
    The discharge tubeA short elbow bolts to the side of the disposal and runs to the trap. Its gasket must sit flat, and the tube must slope slightly downhill.
  • 3
    The dishwasher inletA small spout on the side takes the dishwasher drain hose. It ships blocked, which surprises people.
  • 4
    The power supplyMost units plug into an outlet under the sink or connect to a switch. This is the one part where a mistake is not just messy.

Knocking out the dishwasher plug

Inside the dishwasher inlet sits a thin plastic disc, the knockout plug. Manufacturers leave it in place so a sink-only install does not leak out of the open spout. If you connect a dishwasher and skip this step, the dishwasher cannot drain and you get a standing pool of gray water in the bottom of the tub.

Tap the plug out with a screwdriver and a hammer before the disposal is mounted. Then fish the disc out of the grinding chamber, because a loose plug left inside can jam the first run. People forget this more than any other step, and it is the reason a brand-new disposal sometimes growls on its first use.

High loop or air gap: keeping dishwasher water out of the sink

The dishwasher hose must not let sink water siphon back into the machine. There are two accepted ways to stop that. A high loop lifts the hose up to the underside of the countertop before it drops to the disposal. An air gap is a small fitting on the sink deck that breaks the connection with open air.

The dishwasher's own instructions say which one it expects, and local plumbing rules can add requirements. Washington enforces the 2021 Uniform Plumbing Code, so if you are unsure, ask the permit center before you close up the cabinet.

What a homeowner can do and what to hand off

Reasonable for a careful DIYer

  • Swapping a like-for-like unit on an existing mount
  • Knocking out the dishwasher plug
  • Re-seating the flange with fresh putty
  • Plugging a cord-connected unit into an outlet that already exists

Call a licensed plumber

  • Drain lines that are corroded, loose or sloped the wrong way
  • A cabinet that already shows water damage
  • Adding a disposal where none existed, with new drain work
  • Any wiring that is missing, damaged or needs a new circuit

A disposal needs power, and that part is not a place to improvise. If there is no outlet and no switch, or the existing wiring looks tired, a licensed electrician should handle that piece. We stick to the water side of the work.

How we hook one up, start to finish

  1. 1

    Clear and inspect

    Empty the cabinet, check the old trap and drain arm for corrosion, and look for stains that point to a past leak.

  2. 2

    Mount the unit

    Seat the flange on fresh putty, tighten the mounting ring evenly, and hang the disposal on its lugs.

  3. 3

    Connect the drain

    Attach the discharge tube, join the trap, and connect the dishwasher hose with a high loop or air gap.

  4. 4

    Run and check

    Fill the sink, release it, and watch every joint with a dry paper towel for a slow weep.

That last paper-towel check catches most leaks before they cause damage. If you are reading this because your drain also runs slow, a sluggish line will make a new disposal look faulty, and our drain cleaning crew can clear the line first.

What drives the cost of a hookup

A quote for this job moves with the condition of what is already under the sink. We give a clear price before work starts and we do not guess at a number without seeing the setup.

  • Whether the new unit fits the old mount or needs a different one
  • The state of the trap, drain arm and shutoff valves
  • Whether a dishwasher connection and an air gap are involved
  • Existing power, since wiring is separate licensed work
  • Disposal of the old unit and any cabinet cleanup

Horsepower is a choice, not a plumbing requirement. A larger motor grinds finer and runs quieter, but it does not fix a slow drain on its own.

Disposals in Lakewood: sewer and septic homes are not the same

Sewer in Lakewood is almost entirely provided by Pierce County Public Works and Utilities, so most kitchens drain to the county system. Tillicum and Woodbrook were on septic until county sewer arrived in 2011 and 2012, and some septic systems remain around the area.

If your home is still on septic, talk to your septic service before adding or upgrading a disposal. Ground food solids add to the load the tank has to break down, and septic owners handle all of their own maintenance. Tacoma-Pierce County Health Department approves on-site systems, so it is the right place to ask.
