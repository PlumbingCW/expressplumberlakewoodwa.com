Is Hydro Jetting Safe for Cast Iron Pipes in Lakewood, WA?

A common belief says high-pressure water will wreck an old cast iron drain. That's partly true: pressure can hurt a weak pipe, which is exactly why a plumber looks first.

On sound cast iron, a jetter with the right nozzle and pressure setting can scour off scale that a cable only pokes through. The key is knowing what condition the pipe is in before the water goes on.

By the Lakewood Express Plumbing team. Updated 2026.

What actually builds up inside cast iron

Cast iron rusts from the inside as water and waste sit against the wall. The rust flakes and forms rough lumps called tuberculation, along with layers of scale and grease.

Those lumps narrow the pipe and catch paper and debris. A cable punches a small hole through the middle, so the drain works for a while and then clogs again.

Scale also hides the pipe's true wall thickness. That's why you can't judge a cast iron line by how it drains. A pipe that flows poorly may still be thick and sound, and one that flows fine may be thin.

Why the camera goes in before the jetter

A sewer camera shows cracks, offsets, collapsed sections and how heavy the scaling is. Jetting a pipe with a hidden crack can open the crack or flush debris out through the hole into the soil.

The inspection also sets the jetting plan. Heavy scale calls for a different nozzle and slower passes than light grease. If you want to see the process, ask to watch the footage.

Pressure, nozzles and why settings matter

A jetter isn't one tool. Nozzles throw water forward to open a blockage, backward to pull the hose along, or sideways to scour the pipe wall. A forward-heavy nozzle on a fragile pipe does little good, while a rotating scouring head on sound iron can clear years of scale.

Pressure matters just as much as the nozzle. A plumber working on an old line starts low, watches the camera or the debris coming back, and raises pressure only if the pipe tolerates it. That patience is what separates careful jetting from a risky gamble.

Expect rust-colored water and flakes at the cleanout during the job. That is normal for iron. What you don't want to see is soil or gravel coming back, because that signals a breach in the pipe.

  • Water at the cleanout turns orange-brown: scale is breaking loose.
  • Grease chunks and paper appear: the blockage is clearing.
  • Sand, gravel or soil appears: stop and recheck the pipe with the camera.

What you can do before we arrive

  1. Find your cleanout, usually a capped pipe in the yard, crawlspace or basement, and clear space around it.
  2. Avoid running water in the house once a drain is backing up.
  3. Make a note of which fixtures are slow or gurgling, and for how long.
  4. Tell the plumber if you've used any chemical drain opener, since it affects how the trap is handled.

Those four details help the plumber pick between a cable, a jetter or a camera first. They also keep a simple job simple.

Visible cracks or thin walls

Spot repair or lining may suit better than pressure.

Sagging line holding water

Jetting won't fix a belly. The pipe needs correcting.

A single soft clog

A cable is faster and gentler for a simple blockage.
