Installing a New Toilet in Lakewood, WA, and Where DIY Stops

The old toilet is out in the driveway, the new one is still in its box, and the real question is whether this is an hour of work or a weekend of surprises. Installing a toilet means setting a new bowl over the drain flange on a fresh wax ring, connecting the supply line to the shutoff valve, then leveling and bolting it down.

On a sound flange and a solid floor, plenty of homeowners finish that in an afternoon. The trouble hides under the bowl, so this guide covers what to measure before you buy and which findings should send you to a licensed plumber.

By the Lakewood Express Plumbing team. Updated 2026.

Measure the rough-in before you buy anything

Rough-in is the distance from the finished wall behind the toilet to the center of the drain flange. Twelve inches is the common number, and 10 and 14 inch rough-ins turn up in older bathrooms and remodels. A bowl built for 12 inches will not sit squarely over a 10 inch flange, and the tank will end up crooked against the wall.

Measure to the wall surface, then to the center of the two closet bolts. If baseboard sits behind the toilet, subtract its thickness. Write the number on the box before you leave the store.

  • Bowl shape: round fits tight spaces, elongated gives about two inches more seat length and projects farther into the room.
  • Tank label: check the gallons per flush stamped inside the tank, since it affects how well the bowl clears.
  • Bowl height: standard and comfort height differ by a few inches, and the choice is yours.
  • Supply connection: a 3/8 inch compression shutoff is typical, but confirm what is on your wall.

The flange decides how hard this job will be

The flange is the ring on the floor that the bowl bolts to and that the wax ring seals against. It should sit on top of the finished floor or flush with it, not sunk below a new layer of tile or vinyl. A flange that is too low leaves a gap the wax cannot fill, and that gap is where slow leaks begin.

Look for cracks, rust-eaten metal and slots that no longer hold the closet bolts. A cracked plastic or cast flange can sometimes take a repair ring, but one that has rotted loose from the floor needs real repair work.

Also check the floor around it. Press a screwdriver tip into the wood beside the flange, and if it sinks in easily the subfloor is wet or rotted and the toilet should not go back on top of it.

The swap in order

  1. 1

    Shut off and empty

    Close the quarter-turn valve on the wall, flush, then sponge the last water out of the tank and bowl. Disconnect the supply line over a towel because a cup of water is still in it.

  2. 2

    Lift out the old bowl

    Remove the two nuts at the base and rock the bowl gently to break the wax seal. Lift with your legs since many toilets weigh 60 to 100 pounds, and set it on cardboard.

  3. 3

    Clean the flange and set the new seal

    Scrape off old wax, stuff a rag in the pipe to block sewer gas, and fit the new closet bolts. Place the wax ring on the flange, or on the horn of the bowl, but not both.

  4. 4

    Lower, bolt, connect, test

    Lower the bowl straight down onto the bolts, press with your body weight, then tighten the nuts a little at a time. Connect the supply, open the valve slowly and flush several times.

Alternate between the two base nuts in small turns. Cranking one side down fully is how porcelain cracks, and a cracked base cannot be repaired.

Leveling, shimming and the dry-tissue leak test

Set a small level across the bowl rim, then front to back. If the floor is uneven, plastic shims under the bowl fix it, and you trim them flush once the toilet is tight. Do not tighten the bolts to pull a rocking toilet flat, because that stresses the porcelain.

After the first flushes, dry the base and the supply connection, then wrap a square of toilet tissue around each joint. Check it again after an hour. Any damp spot at the base means the wax seal failed, and the toilet has to come up again before the water reaches the subfloor.

Hand the job to a licensed plumber when you find this

Flange below the floor, cracked or loose

A repair ring or a flange replacement is plumbing work, and a poor repair leaks under the floor where you cannot see it.

Soft or stained subfloor

Rot around the base means a long-running leak. The wood has to be dealt with before any toilet goes back on top.

A shutoff valve that will not close

Forcing a seized valve can break it and leave you without a way to stop the water. Shutting the main is the fallback.

Moving the toilet to a new spot

A new location needs a new drain and vent path. That belongs under plumbing installation, not a weekend swap.

Slow drain behind the toilet

If the bowl was sluggish before the swap, a new bowl will not fix a clogged line. Our toilet repair and installation team can check the drain first.

Toilet work under Lakewood and Washington rules

Washington enforces the 2021 Uniform Plumbing Code with state amendments, in effect statewide since March 15, 2024. If you buy a bowl at a big-box store, the labels and installation instructions are written to that code, so follow them rather than a video from another state.

The City of Lakewood says it is not a full-service permit agency, and its older building FAQ says a permit is required before installing, altering, repairing or replacing a plumbing system. Whether a like-for-like toilet swap counts is a question to settle with the city permit center before you start.

Anyone doing plumbing work for hire in Washington must be certified by the state Department of Labor and Industries. You can check a plumber on L&I's online verify tool before you let anyone into the bathroom.

What the rules say about the toilet you buy

Federal rules cap new toilets at 1.6 gallons per flush, and many models carry the EPA WaterSense label at 1.28 gallons or less. Check the rough-in distance before you shop, because the toilet has to fit it.

The Uniform Plumbing Code (UPC), 2021 edition, is the code Washington enforces, so if the drain or vent needs changes, that work may call for a permit.
