How Often Should You Flush a Water Heater in Lakewood, WA?

That rumbling or popping from the basement when the heater runs is usually sediment. Mineral bits settle at the bottom of the tank, and the burner or element heats water trapped under them.

How often to flush? Follow your manufacturer's manual, since schedules differ by model. Your water quality changes that rhythm, and this post shows how to judge it.

By the Lakewood Express Plumbing team. Updated 2026.

What the manual says and what changes the answer

The manual that came with your heater is the authority on timing. Many list an annual flush, but yours may say otherwise, and a model with a self-cleaning feature may differ.

  • Heavier mineral content in your water means more sediment, so flush more often.
  • Heavy daily use moves more water through the tank.
  • An older tank usually holds more built-up sediment.
  • If you've never flushed it, start gently since stuck sediment can clog the drain valve.

What sediment does inside the tank

Sediment forms an insulating layer on the tank floor. On a gas heater, the burner has to push heat through it, which wastes fuel and stresses the steel. On an electric heater, buried lower elements overheat and fail early.

Signs include popping, a rumble as steam bubbles escape, slower recovery and cloudy or gritty hot water. None of these alone proves sediment, but together they point there.

How a flush works

  1. 1

    Power down and close the cold valve

    Turn off the breaker or set a gas heater to pilot or off per the manual. Close the cold supply valve.

  2. 2

    Connect a hose

    Attach it to the drain valve and run it to a drain or outdoors, away from people. The water will be hot.

  3. 3

    Drain and agitate

    Open a hot tap upstairs, open the drain valve and let the tank empty. Briefly opening the cold valve stirs sediment loose.

  4. 4

    Refill and restart

    Close the drain, refill with a hot tap open until water runs steady, then restore power or gas.

Refilling before power is the rule that saves elements. If you aren't comfortable with gas controls, hand it to a licensed plumber.

The anode rod question

Inside most tanks, a sacrificial anode rod corrodes so the steel doesn't. Flushing is a natural moment to ask whether it's due for a check, since a used-up rod lets the tank itself corrode.

Checking or replacing the rod takes a wrench and often some force. If the rod is stuck or the tank is old, a plumber can judge whether it is worth the effort.

A simple check you can do between flushes

Once or twice a year, run a bucket of water from the drain valve for a minute and look at it. Clear water means little sediment is leaving the tank. Water with grit, flakes or a cloudy tint says sediment is building.

Also look at the tank base and fittings for damp spots or rust trails. Moisture there points to a leak that a flush won't solve. Listen too: a new rumble that wasn't there last season is information.

Write the date of each flush on the tank or in your phone. Over a few years that record shows how fast your own tank collects sediment, which beats any general rule.

When flushing will not help

Flushing may help with

  • Popping or rumbling
  • Cloudy, gritty hot water
  • Slow recovery on a regularly used tank

Flushing will not fix

  • A leaking tank or wet floor underneath
  • Rusty-brown hot water from corroded steel
  • A failed element, thermostat or pilot
  • Cold water from a tank that never heats

A tank that is leaking from the body can't be repaired and flushing may stress it. In that case, water heater repair won't be the answer and replacement is likely.
