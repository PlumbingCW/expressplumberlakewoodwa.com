Shut it down first, then look

Never put your hand into a disposal, even when it is switched off. The blades are sharp, and a jammed motor can release without warning. Use tongs or pliers to remove anything you can see.

  1. Flip the wall switch to off.
  2. Turn off the circuit breaker for the unit if you can find it.
  3. Stop running the faucet and the dishwasher, since water has nowhere to go.
  4. Look for a leak under the sink and put a bucket under it.
  5. Call (253) 275-5116 if water is spreading or the sink is full.

A flooded cabinet near an outlet is an electrical risk. Keep clear of any wet cord, and do not stand in water to reach a switch or panel.

Two or more of these together usually point toward a unit that has reached the end of its life.

A jammed or humming disposal

A hum with no spin means the motor is trying to turn something that is stuck. Most units have a hex socket in the center of the underside. A plumber inserts a hex key, usually a quarter-inch, and works it back and forth to free the flywheel.

Once the jam is cleared, the red reset button on the underside pops back in. If it trips again within a day, the motor is wearing out. A unit older than ten years that jams twice is cheaper to replace than to keep repairing.

Three places a disposal leaks

The top flange

Water at the sink rim or dripping from the mounting ring. The putty seal around the drain opening has dried out, and the fix is to reseat the flange.

The dishwasher hose

A wet spot at the small side inlet. A clamp may be loose or the rubber hose is cracked.

The drain outlet

Drips from the bolted elbow connecting the unit to the trap. A new gasket often solves it.

The housing itself

Water coming through the body means corrosion inside. That is not repairable.

To trace a leak, dry everything, then run water for a few minutes and wipe each joint with a paper towel. The wet paper tells you which joint is guilty. If you cannot find it, our leak detection page explains how we trace a slow one.

What not to put down the disposal

Avoid

  • Grease, fat or oil, which cool into a paste in the trap
  • Coffee grounds, eggshells and potato peels in quantity
  • Pasta, rice and bread that swell when wet
  • Fibrous scraps such as celery strings and corn husks
  • Bones, fruit pits and glass

Fine with plenty of cold water

  • Soft scraps in small amounts
  • Citrus peels from time to time
  • Ice cubes, which knock residue off the walls

A disposal is not a trash can. Waste that does not break down ends up in the trap, then in the drain line. If you have already clogged the line, see our drain cleaning page.

Repair or replace: the honest math

  • Repair a jam, a loose hose clamp, a seal or the plumbing underneath.
  • Replace a unit with a corroded housing, a dead motor, or blades that spin but do not grind.
  • Replace when the unit is more than a decade old and has needed work twice.
  • Consider a higher-horsepower model if you grind a lot of scraps, but pick the size that fits the cabinet.

We say when a repair is not worth it, and quote the replacement price before we remove anything.

Dishwashers, air gaps and drain connections

A dishwasher drains into the side inlet on the disposal, or into the sink tailpiece if there is no disposal. A new disposal has a knockout plug in that inlet. If the plug is not removed, the dishwasher cannot drain, and dirty water backs up into its tub.

Some sinks have an air gap, a small chrome cylinder on the deck. It keeps sink water from running backward into the dishwasher. If it gurgles or leaks during the cycle, the cause is often a clog in the short hose, not the cylinder itself.

On septic? Ask before you install

Tillicum and Woodbrook were on septic until county sewer arrived in 2011 and 2012. Some properties in Pierce County still use septic systems, and a disposal adds solids that fill a tank sooner. If you are not sure whether your home is on sewer or septic, check with Pierce County or ask the Tacoma-Pierce County Health Department before adding a unit.

Tell us your sewer status when you call so we can plan the work. That applies in Lakewood and in neighboring towns such as University Place, where some areas still lack sewer.

Trap rules behind the disposal layout

The Uniform Plumbing Code (UPC) requires a trap on every fixture drain, which is why a disposal drains into a trap and not straight into the wall. The trap holds a water seal that keeps sewer gas out of the kitchen.

If a plumber moves or replaces the disposal, the trap and the drain arm should end up properly sloped toward the wall.
