Cottages, ranchers and townhomes: what each University Place era asks of its plumbing

Older Census figures put the median University Place home at 1978. About 16 percent of homes were built before 1960, about 37 percent between 1960 and 1979, and about 36 percent between 1980 and 1999. The city's housing element also notes a stock of small bungalows and cottages built through the 1940s.

That spread changes what we bring to a call. Where a cottage has gained a bathroom or a kitchen over the decades, old and newer work can meet inside one wall. A 1990s house is more likely to be on a second or third water heater, so we ask the age on the tank label before anything else.

Our dispatch line runs around the clock for University Place, so a flooded laundry room at 3 a.m. gets the same answer as a dripping faucet at noon.

1940s cottages and bungalows

Small footprints mean the main shutoff and the water heater often share one tight utility corner. Tell us where it is and we can plan the route in.

1960s and 70s houses

1980s and 90s builds

Townhomes and multifamily

About 36 percent of homes are multifamily by the older count. In shared buildings a leak can be your neighbor's, so the building manager belongs in the first call.

Sewer or septic: confirm your line before the work starts

The city says sewer is available city-wide, but some residential areas are still unsewered. It runs a sewer-extension program funded by ARPA money on 67th Ave W, on the stretch from 57th Ave to Cirque Dr, and on 36th St W off Grandview Dr W. Wastewater is treated at Pierce County's Chambers Creek plant.

Why it matters to you: a backup means one thing on a sewered street and another on a septic lot. On septic, the fix may involve a tank or drain field and the Tacoma-Pierce County Health Department, not only a cleared pipe. Ask the city whether your street is sewered or in the extension area, and say so when you call.

If you are on public sewer, Pierce County guidance says the homeowner looks after the line from the house to the public line. Ask the city how that applies at your address. When the backup is in your line, drain cleaning or a sewer line repair is the usual path.

Permits: ask the city before assuming

The city's Planning and Development Services handles building, plumbing and mechanical permits generally, and you can reach it at 253-566-5656. We found no specific water heater rule, so ask the city whether your swap needs a permit and an inspection.

Washington enforces the 2021 Uniform Plumbing Code, and state code requires water heaters to be strapped. Anyone doing plumbing for hire must be certified by the state Department of Labor and Industries, and you can check any plumber on L&I's online verification tool.
