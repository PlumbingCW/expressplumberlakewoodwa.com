Sewer Camera Inspection in Lakewood, WA

A sewer camera inspection in Lakewood, WA sends a waterproof camera on a flexible rod through your sewer line and records what it sees. You get to watch the inside of a pipe you normally never see.

What the camera sees inside the pipe

The camera head is about the size of a small flashlight, with lights around the lens. It travels down the line on a push rod while the plumber watches a monitor. A transmitter in the head can be picked up above ground, which lets the plumber mark where each feature sits.

The picture shows pipe material, wall condition, joints, water level, debris and anything growing into the line. It is the only way to see a problem under a lawn or slab without digging.

    Is the clog a one-off or a pattern?Footage of the pipe wall shows whether grease, roots or damage keep causing blockages.
  • 2
    Cleaning or repair?A clear, intact pipe needs cleaning. A cracked or sagging one needs repair.
  • 3
    Lining or digging?The camera shows whether the pipe is open and straight enough for a liner.

Before you buy: the sewer line is part of the house

A general home inspection does not typically include running a camera through the sewer line. Many buyers only learn about a root-filled or sagging line after moving in. A camera look before closing can show a defect worth far more than the visit, and the recording is useful in negotiation.

About 42 percent of Lakewood housing is in buildings with three or more units, and 55 percent of households rent, per recent Census survey figures. Landlords and property managers also use camera inspections when taking on a building, to see what the line looks like before a tenant reports a backup.

If the recording shows a defect, the next step may be sewer line repair or a cleaning. If the home is on the older side, ask the seller what they know about past backups.

Reading the footage: belly, roots, offsets

A belly

A sag in the pipe where water pools. It shows as a standing puddle on camera and tends to collect solids.

Root intrusion

Fine white hairs or thick tendrils coming through a joint or crack. Roots seek moisture and nutrients inside the line.

Offset joint

Two sections no longer line up, leaving a ledge that catches debris.

Cracks and fractures

Lines or breaks along the wall, sometimes with soil visible behind them.

Scale and grease

Coatings that narrow the pipe and signal a need for cleaning or jetting.

A belly can only be repaired by digging and relaying the pipe at the right slope. Roots and offsets can sometimes be handled with cutting, lining or a spot repair, depending on how bad they are.

When clogs keep coming back

A line that clogs every few months has a reason, and a camera finds it. Wet ground in a climate with about 42 inches of rain a year keeps roots active, so a line near mature trees is a common suspect. If the footage shows only coated walls, hydro jetting is usually the better fix than another cable pass.

  3. 3Marked locationsIf you ask, the plumber paints or flags the line's path and the spots that matter.

If you need the recording for a seller, an insurer or another contractor, ask for it before the plumber leaves.

What a camera cannot do

  • It cannot see through a pipe that is completely blocked or collapsed. A line may need clearing first.
  • It cannot tell you the age of the pipe, only its material and condition.
  • It cannot show a leak that is tight or intermittent. Some leaks need a separate leak detection method, covered by our leak detection service.
  • It cannot inspect a line without access. Without a cleanout, the plumber may have to pull a toilet.
  • It does not fix anything. Repair, lining or cleaning is a separate step.

Where an inspection leads

Most inspections end in one of three places. The line is fine and you move on with the recording on file. The line is dirty but sound, and the plumber books cleaning, as described on our sewer line cleaning page. The line is damaged, and you decide between lining and digging with the footage in front of you.

  • AccessA cleanout makes the job quick. Pulling a toilet adds labor.

How inspection findings get graded

Utilities and many contractors grade pipe defects with the coding system from NASSCO, called PACP. A report that names defect types, their position along the line and their severity is far more useful than one that says the line looks bad.

Ask for the footage and the written notes, and ask what each finding means for the next five years. A camera pass proves what is inside the pipe, but only the notes explain what to do about it.
